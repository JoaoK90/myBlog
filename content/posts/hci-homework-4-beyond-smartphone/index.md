I already wear glasses. Imagining that the same frames could one day replace my phone feels like a natural next step: one less object to carry, with information available where I am looking.

My bet is that, within the next decade, we could stop carrying a separate smartphone and move its everyday functions into our glasses. The hardware is still catching up with that idea. But the question that interests me most is what I would actually want to do with it. As an international student living away from my family, I have a very personal answer to that.

## From a room to the frames on my face

The history of computing makes that prediction tempting. **ENIAC, unveiled in 1946**, occupied a large room, roughly **167 square metres**. By the late 1970s, personal computers were reaching homes; the **IBM PC of 1981** is a familiar example of computing that fitted on a desk.

{{< homework-photo src="eniac-1946.jpg" alt="ENIAC and members of its team surrounded by large cabinets of electronics, around 1946." src2="ibm-pc-1981.jpg" alt2="An IBM PC with its monitor, desktop system unit and keyboard." caption="ENIAC, around 1946, and the IBM PC, 1981. Images: [Computer History Museum](https://www.computerhistory.org/chess/stl-42fa8835902f1/) and [CHM's 1981 timeline](https://www.computerhistory.org/timeline/1981/)." >}}

Portable computers followed in the 1980s, gradually taking on the laptop shape we recognise. **IBM's PC Convertible, released in 1986**, is one example. Mobile phones developed alongside them; smartphones later combined more computing tasks in a pocket-sized device. The **iPhone introduced in 2007** brought calls, internet access and media behind a multitouch screen.

{{< homework-photo src="ibm-pc-convertible-1986.jpg" alt="IBM's PC Convertible laptop with its screen open and an accessory printer beside it." src2="iphone-2007.jpg" alt2="Steve Jobs holding the original iPhone at its introduction in 2007." caption="A laptop form and a pocket-sized touchscreen computer. Images: [IBM](https://www.ibm.com/history/personal-computer) and [Apple](https://www.apple.com/newsroom/2017/01/iphone-at-ten-the-revolution-continues/)." >}}

Size changed where we could use a computer. A laptop let us carry a workspace; a smartphone made maps and messages available while walking. Software controls let the same screen become a keyboard, map or video player. A larger screen helps us read but becomes harder to carry or reach across with one hand.

Glasses could change that relationship again. The display would no longer have to fit inside the rectangle I can comfortably hold.

### The computer behind the glasses

I briefly covered **Meta VR Glasses** in my [post about Sutherland's vision]({{< relref "posts/hci-homework-2-ultimate-display" >}}). [Marques Brownlee's hands-on video](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_x58r9TBois) gives a better sense of what using them is like. He liked the sharp displays and the remarkably light design. Visually, I also find this much more appealing than the bulky shape of the Apple Vision Pro.

There is an important detail behind that smaller shape. The glasses weigh about **100 grams**, while a separate, cable-connected **puck contains the processing hardware, storage and battery**.

{{< figure src="marques-brownlee-meta-vr-thumbnail.jpg" link="https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=_x58r9TBois" target="_blank" rel="noopener" alt="Thumbnail of Marques Brownlee wearing Meta VR Glasses. Click to watch the video." caption="Marques Brownlee's Meta VR Glasses video." class="homework-photo" >}}

These are not ordinary transparent glasses. Cameras provide a video view of the surroundings, with digital content added. Eye and hand tracking provide the controls.

One demonstration I particularly liked was mapping a keyboard and trackpad onto a real desk. The user touches a physical surface instead of tapping floating keys, which Brownlee describes as much more satisfying. Even a virtual interface benefits from having something real under our fingers.

Meta announced the device in **September 2026**, with sales planned for **spring 2027**. It is an encouraging intermediate step towards the glasses I imagine, rather than an everyday pair that already contains an entire phone. [Meta's announcement](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/09/introducing-meta-vr-glasses-3d-movies-immersive-live-sports-100-grams/).

## Useful without taking over

I can imagine a later step combining those displays with the kind of brain–computer interface discussed in my previous post. A deliberately chosen intention could become an input, without needing a hand gesture. The glasses would provide the output in front of us.

{{< homework-photo src="meta-vr-glasses.jpg" alt="Meta VR Glasses connected to their separate computing puck." src2="neuralink-implant.png" alt2="A Neuralink implant held between two fingers, with its electrode threads visible." caption="Left: Meta VR Glasses ([Meta](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/09/introducing-meta-vr-glasses-3d-movies-immersive-live-sports-100-grams/)). Right: a Neuralink implant, pictured in my earlier post." >}}

For example, imagine wanting a recipe for your child's birthday cake. A search assistant could bring a suitable recipe into view beside the counter, moving it away when it obscures something important. Or imagine choosing a destination and having a route appear along the street, without taking out a phone. That would extend the navigation idea I explored in [my paper-prototype post]({{< relref "posts/hci-homework-3-ar-navigation" >}}).

This is my prediction about a possible interface. Current Neuralink research decodes specific intended actions or attempted speech through trained systems. But its current focus is clinical research for people with major movement or communication impairments.

### What Weiser meant by disappearing

In **1991**, Mark Weiser's [*The Computer for the 21st Century*](https://graphics.stanford.edu/courses/cs428-03-spring/Papers/readings/General/Weiser_SciAm91.htm) imagined computing integrated into the environment and everyday activities. Its disappearance was also about attention: people could focus on what they were doing instead of continually operating a computer. He described small tabs, medium-sized pads and large boards, cooperating at different scales.

My residence card is a small example to think with: it gives me access to a building without opening a computer application. Yet the confusing readers I described in my first post show how the technology can still demand attention. We have pieces of this future around us, with uneven results.

For me, the challenge is making systems cooperate reliably and respond appropriately to context. A recipe should remain available without covering the task; a route should appear when I want directions. Weiser also discusses privacy, especially relevant to an interface close to our bodies and intentions.

### Would I want my fridge making the grocery list?

The connected refrigerator makes me less optimistic about assuming that every useful action should become automatic. Samsung's **Family Hub and Bespoke AI** products offer a concrete example of appliances taking on more of the household routine.

For its **2025 AI Vision Inside 2.0** system, Samsung describes camera-based food recognition, including **37 types of fruit and vegetables**, with additional registration options for packaged foods. The resulting inventory can support recipe suggestions and a shopping list to which used-up items are added. Recognition has limits and some items still need manual registration.

{{< homework-photo src="samsung-family-hub.png" alt="A Samsung Bespoke refrigerator with a large AI Family Hub touchscreen on its upper-right door." caption="Bespoke AI Family Hub refrigerator. Image: [Samsung](https://www.samsung.com/us/explore/family-hub-refrigerator/overview/)." >}}

That is more specific than a fridge deciding and buying everything on its own. Samsung's announced Instacart integration lets users replenish groceries through the refrigerator screen in supported US areas.

I suspect that control matters more than we sometimes assume. A missing ingredient does not necessarily mean I want to buy it again. Maybe I want a different meal. I would welcome a suggested list that I can review, while still wanting the final decision to be mine. I would even be losing control of my own kitchen. To what extent do we need AI to do things for us?

Cost and privacy also make this convenience less attractive. I don't want the personal agent that spies on me to know even what is inside my fridge.

## Three reasons to keep wearing them

Imagine now that mixed-reality headsets are as common as smartphones. These are three applications I think people would return to, starting with the one that matters most to me. The sketches show imagined interactions.

### Having my family beside me

As an international student, I miss the people I left behind. I miss my family a lot. Being able to see them through a phone matters, but I keep imagining how different it would feel to see someone in front of me at their normal height, with a face I could look at from more than one angle.

I picture washing the dishes while my mum stands beside me and talks. We would not need a special occasion or a meeting agenda. We could share a very ordinary moment. That is the part I want most: having some of everyday life together again, even while living far apart.

It would still be a digital representation, but a convincing sense of presence would mean much more to me than a video floating in a rectangle. If it could give me that feeling of having them here, I would be willing to pay whatever it took.

{{< homework-photo src="Sketch1.png" alt="Concept montage of a point-cloud person beside someone washing dishes." caption="Family Presence: a conversation alongside an everyday activity." >}}

Meta already advertises [**Hologram Calling**](https://about.fb.com/news/2026/09/introducing-meta-vr-glasses-3d-movies-immersive-live-sports-100-grams/), so this direction is more than my own wish. What I am imagining is how convincing and comfortable that presence could become in daily use.

### Working on the same thing from different places

A second application would build on spatial meetings: **a shared workbench** where people discuss the same document or 3D model. As an engineering student, I can imagine the value of pointing to a particular part of a model while another person sees exactly what I mean.

{{< homework-photo src="Sketch2.png" alt="Concept montage of a meeting with holographic participants and a shared 3D car model." caption="Shared Workbench: discussing a 3D model together." >}}

People could select, rotate and mark the shared object, with everyone's changes visible. I would expect repeat use in classes and company meetings when a spatial view makes an explanation easier.

### A cinema I can comfortably use in bed

I have already tried watching a film while lying in bed with an Oculus headset, imagining a cinema screen in front of me. It did not work out: the device was heavy and uncomfortable, and the resolution was too low for the experience I wanted.

If lighter glasses could make that comfortable, I would be very interested. I could stay at home, lie down and have a large screen to myself without finding room for a large television.

{{< homework-photo src="Sketch3.png" alt="Concept montage of a person lying in bed watching a large virtual cinema screen." caption="My Own Cinema: a large screen while lying in bed." >}}

People could use this whenever they watch a film, provided the display and comfort hold up for its full length. I would want to reposition the screen from where I am lying and pause without sitting up to find a controller.

Of these three, entertainment would be easy for me to imagine using. But seeing my family beside me is the strongest reason. A smaller computer is an impressive engineering achievement; sharing an ordinary conversation with my mum while I wash the dishes is what would make that achievement personal.
